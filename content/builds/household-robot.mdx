## Overview

A small Wi-Fi robot that lives with the family. It should know each person and the pets, talk with
each of them in a way that suits them, remember them and the house, keep to the household's rules
about where it may go and when, and take remote driving. This is lab experiment 3.

- **v1** is the rolling robot. It drives on carpet and hard floor without hitting anything,
  converses, recognises people and pets, remembers, follows the household's rules and goes where
  it is told. It ends with a vision-language-action policy trained on its own driving.
- **v2** adds an arm to pick up light toys and put them away.

Where it stands: everything for the chassis and the electronics deck is printed: the base plate, both wheels with their TPU tires, the servo
cradles, the rear caster and the three deck trays. With everything laid out on the plate, the
wheel mounts failed: under the robot's weight each servo cradle rolls over and its wheel leans
in. The fix is a stiffer wheel module, a boxed servo mount on a wide foot with a beam tying left
and right together (see the servo mount and the lessons below). Then come the power wiring, the
Raspberry Pi and real wheels. A control app for a phone is built and drives simulated wheels.
Nothing runs against hardware yet.

## Design

Two driven wheels and one swivel caster at the back. That works on carpet and hard floor and keeps
the base simple.

- **Drive.** The lab's two spare STS3215 12V servos, in wheel mode, turn printed wheels. The
  estimate is about 0.28 m/s at 12V; it gets measured on the bench before anything else.
- **Compute.** A Raspberry Pi 5 on the robot runs the motors, the sensors and the safety gate. The
  Mac does speech-to-text, recognition, vision and policy inference over Wi-Fi. Conversation goes
  through the Claude API.
- **Power.** A 12.8V 10Ah LiFePO4 pack (about 128 Wh) with its own BMS. A main switch and fuse,
  then an INA226 fuel gauge. The motor rail runs through the e-stop, a 12V buck-boost and its own
  fuse; the compute rail is a 5V buck to the Pi. The e-stop cuts motor power only, so the Pi stays
  up and logs what happened.
- **Sensing.** Three forward time-of-flight sensors in a soft TPU bumper, two bumper switches, and
  downward time-of-flight cliff sensors. Infrared reflectance sensors mistake dark carpet for a
  drop, so they are out. A front camera now, and a 2D LiDAR later for mapping.
- **Audio.** A reSpeaker mic array on top of the head and a small speaker.
- **Privacy mode.** A mic mute and a printed camera shutter that the gate can see, and a light that
  shows when the robot is listening.

### Who it knows

Recognition and memory run on the lab machine and the robot, and their data stays in a local store
by default. Faces and voices are recognised together, on device, from live frames that are then
dropped. Anyone the robot does not recognise with confidence is treated as a guest.

After a conversation the robot writes short notes, not transcripts or audio, and anyone can say
"forget that". The household's rules (named rooms, no-go zones, rooms closed at certain times,
quiet hours) live on the map and are enforced by the safety gate. They change only on the lab
machine, never by voice alone.

What gets saved, shared or published is the owner's call
([ADR 0013](https://github.com/sol-dev-44/physical-ai-lab/blob/main/docs/adr/0013-owner-governs-personal-data.md)).

## Servo mount

Each wheel servo sits in a printed cradle that holds it from below. Its top mounting holes sit only
about 2.5 mm under the spinning hub, which leaves no room for a plate, a screw head and the 2 mm of
air the hub needs. The bottom holes are free, and that is how the SO-101 arm holds its servos.

<CradleViews />

The cradle is a U. The back wall carries four <Val k="cradle.pad_mm" digits="1" /> pads for the
servo to sit on, and one opening for the idler disc, the two bus connectors and the raised pad on
the servo's bottom. The cheeks hug the case <Val k="design.cradle.side_clearance_mm" digits="1" />
clear, and the lower cheek is the foot, with an <Val k="design.cradle.ear_screw" /> ear at each
end.

The servos came with bags of M2 x 6 self-tapping screws. They go <Val k="cradle.screw_depth_mm" />
into the case, short of the <Val k="servo.case.max_screw_depth_mm" /> its holes allow. The wheel
axle ends up <Val k="cradle.axle_above_foot_mm" /> above the foot, which is the number the base
plate needs.

Tests load the vendored servo model into the cradle and check for clashes; the check fails, as it
should, on a servo lowered 0.6 mm. Both cradles are printed and fitted: each servo slides in, sits
flat on its pads and holds solid. The loose metal idler disc on the servo's bottom comes off first
and goes with the spares.

The fit was right, but the cradle was not stiff enough. With the deck laid out on the plate, the
cradles rolled over under the robot's weight (see "The hinge with two bolts" below). The
replacement keeps the same screws into the servo. It adds:

- a box round the whole case;
- a foot almost as wide as the gap to the battery, with six bolts on two lines;
- a print orientation that keeps the bending along the layers;
- a beam across the robot joining the two wheel mounts.

A bearing that carries the wheel, so the servo only drives, follows before the arm.

<ServoSheet />

The servo's numbers come from two places. The vendored STS3215 model, from The Robot Studio's
SO-ARM100 repository (Apache-2.0), matched every caliper check on the case and its holes, so it is
trusted for the case. The horn on the lab's servos differs from the model by 0.3 to 0.5 mm, so the
hub keeps its own measured numbers.

### Height and the caster

The caster's mounting height is not published anywhere, so it was measured when it arrived: 50 mm
from the floor to the top of its plate. That settled the base. The cradles sit on top of the
plate, whose top face is 44.4 mm off the floor, and the caster bolts up into a small printed cup
that stands on the plate's top face. The cup is a separate part so the plate prints flat. Its
cavity is a box for the caster's plate joined to a circle for the fork, which reaches 24 mm from
the swivel axis as it turns. The fork swings through a matching hole in the plate.

A test adds the heights up to the floor (plate top, cup, caster) so a part that sits on another
cannot be sized from the wrong face again. The corrected cup is 8.6 mm tall, and with it fitted
the robot sits level.

## Control app

The robot serves its own control app on the home network: open a link on a phone and drive with a
thumb stick, or later with an Xbox controller paired to the Pi. It is never reachable from the
internet; remote driving comes in phase 4 over a private VPN.

Driving is a dead-man. The phone holds a lease on the wheels that lasts 300 ms and renews it with
a heartbeat every 100 ms while the stick is held. A watchdog checks every 50 ms and stops the
wheels when the lease runs out, so letting go, closing the tab or losing Wi-Fi all stop the robot.
Stop is a tool the safety gate never refuses. One phone drives at a time, and a phone pairs with a
link that carries a one-time token.

The first version runs on simulated wheels. Each piece had a written spec and a code review, and
the reviews found about a dozen ways the wheels could have been left turning (a refused, dropped
or failed command, a reconnect, a second phone) before any real wheel was attached. Next it gets
real wheels. The open question there is what a wheel-mode bus servo does if the robot's software
dies: it may keep turning, so the servos' own comms-loss behaviour, or a separate watchdog or
power cut, gets settled first.

## Rules and safety

- **One path to the motors, on the robot.** A lab-agent on the Pi is the only path to the motors,
  and losing Wi-Fi means stop. The decision record gets written before phase 1.
- **Sensor rules are interlocks.** The bumpers, the forward and cliff sensors, the speed cap and the
  battery check run as interlocks in the safety gate, and so will the household's rules once there
  is a map.
- **The e-stop cuts motor power only.** The Pi stays up and logs what happened.
- **Remote driving goes over a private VPN (Tailscale), not the website.** The website stays
  queue-only
  ([ADR 0003](https://github.com/sol-dev-44/physical-ai-lab/blob/main/docs/adr/0003-web-never-touches-hardware.md)):
  it can ask, and the agent on the robot decides.
- **The arm moves light toys only.** In v2 it puts toys into a bin, and an interlock stops it
  whenever a person or a pet is within reach.
- **Personal data is the owner's call.** The wake word and speech-to-text run locally by default.
  Nothing personal goes in the repo or on this site unless the owner puts it there.

## Lessons

Measure twice, cut once. The first week produced six misses. None cost more than a small print,
and each one changed how the next part gets designed.

### The coupon that nearly touched

The first hub coupon was designed to clear the servo case by 2.5 mm. It cleared it by about two
sheets of paper. The case is low round the shaft, but a raised block beside the horn sits only
2.0 mm below the horn's top, and the coupon's recess round the horn left only 0.5 mm above it. The hub
was redesigned to sit flat on the horn's top face. It now clears by
<Val k="wheel.case_gap_mm" digits="1" />, the new minimum, and a second coupon proved the fit before
the full hubs were printed.

### The holes on the other side

A bracket was pitched for the servo's top mounting holes before anyone checked the rest of the
case. The bottom has its own four holes, the two bus connectors, a step and a raised pad, and the
top holes sit too close under the spinning hub to use. A full survey of the servo model followed,
and the cradle now mounts from below.

### The measurement nobody could take

The tire needed the printed rim's seat diameter, and it was asked for even though caliper jaws
cannot reach across a <Val k="wheel.rim_od_mm" digits="0" /> seat with the flanges in the way. The
fix was a reading that can actually be taken: across the top flange's flat land, minus the two
flanges. The tire's bore is sized to that.

### The fork nobody measured

The first caster cup was printed knowing that the fork's sweep was not published anywhere, on
the idea that the print would be the test. The caster's plate dropped in and the holes lined up,
but the fork hit the walls as it swivelled. One caliper reading (24 mm from the swivel axis) would
have caught it. The second cup has a round cavity and the fork swings clear all the way round. A
test print checks a fit; it never stands in for a number nobody measured.

### The plate counted twice

With the chassis assembled, the robot leaned back about 1.5 degrees. The cup's cavity had been
sized as if the cup started at the plate's underside, but it stands on the top face, so the
plate's 4 mm was counted twice and the caster sat 4 mm high. A test now adds the stacked heights
up to the floor, and the corrected cup sits level.

### The hinge with two bolts

With the deck laid out on the plate, the wheels leaned in. One servo cradle had rolled over
about the inside edge of its foot. Each cradle held the plate with two bolts on a single line,
11 mm from that edge, while the tire pushed up 53 mm out. That is a lever of almost five to one.
The cradle's 2 to 3 mm walls also took the bending across their print layers, the weakest
direction of a printed part, and PETG creeps under a steady load. Every earlier check was about
fit; none asked where the part would tip. The new wheel module puts its bolts on two lines across
a wide foot, prints so the bending runs along the layers, and ties both wheels to one beam.

### The checklist

Since then, a "before designing a part" checklist gates every printed part:

1. Gather every source for each component the part touches: the datasheet, the vendor's CAD model,
   how other builds mount it, and what is already measured.
2. Survey every face, not just the one you plan to use: holes, bosses, steps, connectors, screw
   heads, and anything that moves.
3. List every way to mount it, with what each needs and blocks, and pick one with reasons.
4. For each fastener, check the thread, the hole depth, the screws on hand and room for the driver.
   Size a bolt for about three threads through its nut, not a tip standing proud.
5. Never ask for a measurement the sources already give. When one is needed, say why and check it
   can be taken with the tool on hand.
6. For a part that sits on another, write down which face it sits on and test that the stacked
   heights add up to the floor.
7. Ask whatever is still open in one go, before modelling. A test print checks a fit; it never
   stands in for a number nobody measured.
8. Record what was measured and what was read from a model, so the next part starts from facts.
9. Trace the load path. Name the edge a loaded part would tip about and keep the load's lever to
   the bolts' lever under about two to one. Print so the bending runs along the layers. Tie
   matching left and right loads together. Allow for three times the static load, and for creep.
