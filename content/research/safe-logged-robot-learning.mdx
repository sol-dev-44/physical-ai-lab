## Abstract

Low-cost arms and open policies like ACT put imitation learning on a kitchen table. What usually does
not come with them is the discipline that makes results trustworthy: a single, audited path to the
motors, a record of every command and refusal, and an evaluation that does not depend on someone
remembering how a run went. This write-up describes a harness that provides those three things, the
evidence from three experiments that it works on real hardware, and the protocol for the experiment
that matters most: whether targeted demonstrations fix the placements a learned arm policy misses.

The harness and the first two experiments are complete. The arm study is in progress, and its
results section stays empty until the runs are logged.

## 1. The problem

Robot learning on a budget fails in quiet ways. A policy "works" because the operator remembers the
good runs. A safety limit lives in one script and not in another. A result cannot be checked because
the only record is a video and a feeling. None of this is about the model; it is about the system
around it.

Production software solved the same problems long ago with a few habits: one way in, an append-only
log, tests for every rule, and numbers computed from data instead of typed into a report. The question
here is whether those habits fit a one-person home lab without slowing the research down.

## 2. The harness

<Figure caption="Figure 1. Every request, from a person or a policy, goes through one registry and one gate. Every step, including refusals, is written to one append-only log, and results are computed from that log.">
  <HarnessDiagram />
</Figure>

- **One event contract.** Every run is an append-only JSONL log in one format. It is defined in
  TypeScript and mirrored in Python and Rust; shared fixtures run in all three test suites, and CI checks
  that Python and Rust compute identical summaries from the same runs.
- **One path to hardware.** Every command goes through a typed tool registry and a safety gate: a STOP
  file, an allowlist, argument checks, duration caps, confirmation for risky tools, rate limits, and
  world-state interlocks. Each decision is logged with the rule that made it.
- **Sensors report, the gate decides.** The camera never blocks a move itself. It reports where the robot
  is; interlocks in the gate decide whether a move is allowed, and they are tested like every other rule.
- **The website never touches hardware.** The admin page can only queue a request. The lab machine polls
  for it, and its gate decides. Database policies, pinned by SQL tests, record who asked.
- **Privacy by construction.** Camera frames are tracked and dropped; only numbers are kept. No child ever
  appears in published data (ADR 0007), and published arm clips are hand-reviewed (ADR 0011).

## 3. Evidence so far

**Experiment 0: the harness on real hardware.** A $30 IR blaster drove a toy robot from the command line
and from the website, with every request, decision, send and outcome in the log. The first moves came out
as jerky surges caused by Wi-Fi latency between per-frame sends. Moving timed holds onto the device (one
frame, a repeat count) made motion smooth and closed a gap where a recorded code could outlast the gate's
3 second cap (ADR 0008).

**Experiment 1: a camera as the judge.** An overhead webcam tracked the robot, kept it inside a taped
arena through gate interlocks, and judged every move. In the final run it judged 29 of 29 tracked moves
correctly, tracked a still robot to about 1 mm, and never needed to refuse a move. The lesson that made it
work was to stop reading stickers for heading and infer heading from motion instead.

**Experiment 2: a learned arm policy.** On a Seeed SO-ARM101 Pro pair, an ACT policy trained on 39
teleoperated demonstrations picks a red Lego into a bowl on some placements. The dataset has since grown
to 65 recorded episodes (57,252 frames). Nine
exploratory rollouts of the first policy showed the shape of the problem: grasps on flat and stacked
blocks, misses on every vertical and rotated block. Those placements were rare in the data.

## 4. The arm study

**Question.** When a policy misses a class of placements, do demonstrations that target that class fix it,
without losing what already works?

**Data.** The first policy trained on 39 demonstrations. A second batch of 25 added the under-covered
placements: 6 flat, 6 vertical, 6 rotated (3, 6, 9 and 12 o'clock), 4 stacked and 3 near the bowl or the
mat's edge, with the other two blocks always present as distractors.

**Protocol.**

1. A fixed list of placements, reused for every policy compared.
2. About 10 trials per policy, the same placements in the same order.
3. One outcome per trial: grasped first try, grasped after retries, missed, or dropped, plus the number
   of attempts.
4. Select checkpoints on grasps, never on training loss.

**What the harness adds.** Today these rollouts run in LeRobot outside the gate, with a per-step speed cap
as the only limit, and outcomes are recorded by hand. The next harness step routes rollouts through the
gate and the event log, so that the results below are computed from runs, and the camera judge from
Experiment 1 can score grasps.

## 6. Limitations

- **Small numbers.** About 10 trials per condition can show a large effect, not a small one.
- **One operator, one bench.** Every demonstration comes from one person on one table. The results say
  nothing about other scenes yet.
- **The arm is not gated yet.** Until rollouts go through the harness, the arm's safety rests on the speed
  cap and the operator.
- **No baseline beyond the first policy.** The comparison is within one method (ACT), on purpose.
