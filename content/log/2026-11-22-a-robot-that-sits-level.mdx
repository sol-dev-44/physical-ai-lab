The [household robot](/builds/household-robot) went from parts on a desk to a rolling chassis
this week. Everything structural was modelled in parametric CAD and printed on the lab's H2D.

## The chassis

- **Wheels.** PETG hubs bolt straight to the servos' horns, and TPU 95A tires grip the rims. The
  tire bore was sized to a caliper reading of the printed rim, not the drawing, and both went on
  snug the first time.
- **Servo cradles.** Each wheel servo sits in a cradle that holds it by its bottom holes, because
  the top holes sit too close under the spinning hub. A clash test loads the vendor's servo model
  into the cradle; both cradles fitted solid.
- **Base plate.** One 300 mm PETG plate (4 h 56 min, 223 g) carries the cradles, the battery
  across between the wheels, the caster and hole patterns for everything still to come.

## The lean

On the floor the assembled robot leaned back about 1.5 degrees, toward the caster. The caster
bolts up into a small printed cup that stands on the plate's top face, but the cup's cavity had
been sized as if it started at the plate's underside. The plate's 4 mm was counted twice, so the
caster sat 4 mm too high.

The fix was a shorter cup (8.6 mm, 27 minutes, 10 g) and a test that adds the stacked heights up
to the floor: plate top, cup, caster. The corrected cup went on today and the robot sits level.
The cup's first version had already taught one lesson: it was printed before anyone measured how
far the caster's fork swings, and the fork hit the walls. A test print checks a fit; it never
stands in for a number nobody measured. Both rules are now in the checklist every printed part
goes through.

## The control app

The robot will serve its own control app on the home network. Driving is a dead-man: the phone
holds a 300 ms lease on the wheels and renews it every 100 ms while the stick is held, and a
watchdog stops the wheels when the lease runs out. Stop is a tool the safety gate never refuses.

The first version runs on simulated wheels. Each piece was reviewed, and the reviews found about a
dozen ways the wheels could have been left turning (a refused, dropped or failed command, a
reconnect, a second phone). All were fixed with tests before any real wheel.

## Next

Bench checks on the power parts, then the power wiring, the Raspberry Pi and real wheels. Before
the wheels turn, one open risk gets settled: a wheel-mode bus servo may keep turning if the
robot's software dies.
