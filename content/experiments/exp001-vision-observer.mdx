## Why

In Experiment 0 a person watches every trial and types y or n. That doesn't scale, and it
can't run while nobody is watching. If a cheap camera can judge as well as a person, every
later experiment gets automatic evaluation, and reinforcement learning gets its reward.

## Setup

A 94.5 x 69.5 cm rectangle of duct tape on a wood floor, a webcam looking straight down,
and the toy robot from Experiment 0. Every command goes through the lab's one path to
hardware: tool registry, safety gate, IR blaster. Every step is logged.

## How the camera works

- **Where is the robot?** Whatever differs from a stored picture of the empty arena.
  Colour change and brightness change are counted separately, so shadows don't count; the
  webcam's own exposure shifts are subtracted out; shiny tape is ignored; a region bigger
  than a robot (a hand, a person) reads as "not tracked", which stops motion.
- **Which way is it facing?** Learned from motion. After a forward step the robot faces
  the way it went; after a reverse step, the opposite way.
- **Is the move safe?** Two rules in the safety gate, not in the camera code: no recent
  sighting, no motion; and a move whose predicted end comes within 5 cm of the tape is
  refused. A robot that strays may be moved only if even the worst case brings it back in.
- **Did it work?** The judge compares where the robot was before and after: forward
  means at least 5 cm ahead, reverse at least 5 cm back.
- **Where next?** Each move is picked at random among those predicted to end comfortably
  inside the arena, so the data stays varied and the robot stays put.

<Callout>Frames are processed and dropped. Only numbers are kept, and no image from this experiment is published.</Callout>

## Results

The final run: **30 moves, 29 of 29 tracked moves judged correctly** (the last move lost
the robot, most likely because the toy switched itself off). It stayed in the middle of
the arena, between 37 and 64 cm across and 25 and 55 cm deep. The safety fence never had
to refuse a move, and recovery was never needed. A still robot is tracked to about 1 mm.

| | Forward | Reverse |
|---|---|---|
| Distance per press | about 20 cm | about 18 cm |
| Spread | ±1.6 cm | ±1.6 cm |
| Turn per press | 15 deg right | 11 deg right |

How long the button is held barely matters: every press is one step. So the robot's
real action set is "step forward" and "step back", which makes it a tidy first
reinforcement learning problem.

## What didn't work

- **Stickers for heading.** Green on dark wood vanished. On the head, it swivelled. On the
  body, the head hid it. On the tracks, the readings jumped by up to 110 degrees while the
  robot moved. Every placement was steady when still and unusable in motion.
- **Measuring too eagerly.** Frames that failed to track were silently replaced by the
  last good position, which looked like a robot that teleported or didn't move.
- **A fence that assumed distance = speed x time.** Short presses went four times
  further than predicted, until the motion model got a fixed step per press.

## Status

Concluded as a proof of concept. Not done: the formal person-versus-camera agreement
trials and the null test. The camera, judge and safety rules carry over to the LeRobot
arms in Phase 1.
